
This study set out to examine ideological bias in large language models by looking not only at how often they agree or disagree, but at how they justify those stances across topics, roles, and languages. As we moved across the nine topics, a pattern emerged: some questions seemed to invite a shared framing that held steady no matter how we prompted the models, while others fractured as soon as we shifted the persona. \emph{Multi-party system}—and often \emph{Adopt atheism}—tended to hold together: justifications clustered closely across conditions, as if the models drew on a relatively uniform background narrative. By contrast, \emph{No economic sanctions}, \emph{Compulsory voting}, and \emph{Adopt libertarianism} repeatedly came apart when we introduced national roles; the same prompt in a different voice tugged the reasoning in noticeably different directions. In other words, variation was less a constant “property of the model” and more a topic-sensitive response to how the question was framed.


Persona cues amplified this effect far more than language did. When we compared \emph{Role vs. Assistant} (RvA), divergences in the reasons were consistently larger than either \emph{Native vs. Assistant} (NvA) or \emph{Native vs. Role} (NvR). Switching languages mostly modulated tone and emphasis; assigning a country-specific role re-shaped the stance and its rationale. This held across models, though not equally: GPT-4o-mini was generally steadier, while LLaMA~3.1-70B swung more when the persona changed, suggesting that post-training alignment may dampen these shifts in GPT while leaving LLaMA somewhat freer to follow the role cue. A similar geography appeared across national roles. In our runs, the USA often sat at the high and stable end of the spectrum, Western European countries such as France, Italy, and Germany clustered in a mid–high band, while China, Israel, Iran, and Ukraine tended to be lower and more topic-sensitive—patterns that read like echoes of the cultural framings present in the training data.


Taken together, the two lenses—agreement ratios and embedding-based similarity—tell a complementary story. Agreement counts show where stances line up; the embeddings reveal that even when the surface verdicts converge, the underlying reasons can drift when a persona is applied. For applications that trade on neutrality—journalism, policy analysis, education—this matters: a model may sound fluent in many languages yet deliver role-conditioned reasoning that shifts its ideological center. The upshot is practical as much as theoretical: bias evaluation should remain systematic and transparent, with persona prompts treated as first-class stress tests rather than incidental details, and with continuous monitoring to track how alignment changes the balance between stability and sensitivity over time.
